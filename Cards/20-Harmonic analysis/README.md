# 20 · Harmonic analysis

Prove the convergence theorems for Fourier series and the inversion and Plancherel theorems for the transform, and use convolution, kernels and approximate identities as everyday tools. Derive the probability results that rest on characteristic functions, build and use wavelets, and read a modern harmonic-analysis statement about maximal functions, singular integrals and restriction.

## 01 · Fourier Series in Depth · 9 cards

*Coefficients, kernels, and exactly when and in what sense the series equals the function*

1. Fourier coefficients: each wave's share, read off by an integral that ignores the rest
2. Partial sums are the best fit: least squares in function space, and Bessel's inequality
3. Dirichlet and Fejer kernels: a partial sum is the signal smeared by a fixed shape
4. Dirichlet's theorem: where the series equals the signal, and where it averages the jump
5. Convergence in energy: Parseval, completeness, and Riesz-Fischer
6. Smoothness shows in the tail: how fast coefficients shrink, and how many terms you need
7. Differentiating a series term by term: when it is legal, and what integration costs
8. The Gibbs overshoot and the cure: Fejer averaging and half-range series
9. Carleson's theorem in outline: almost everywhere, the series does converge

## 02 · The Fourier Transform · 10 cards

*The transform of an integrable and of a square-integrable signal, inversion, energy, distributions, and the limits on locality*

1. The Fourier transform of an absolutely integrable signal, and why it fades at infinity
2. The transform rulebook: shifting, scaling, and turning derivatives into multiplication
3. Two pairs worth memorising: gate and sinc, and the bell curve that transforms to itself
4. Fourier inversion: rebuilding the signal from its spectrum, proved with a bell curve
5. Plancherel: the transform preserves energy, and extends to every square-integrable signal
6. Schwartz space and tempered distributions: transforms of spikes, constants and steps
7. The uncertainty principle: no signal is both brief and narrow in frequency
8. Poisson summation and sampling: samples are enough when nothing wiggles too fast
9. The Hilbert transform: shift every frequency a quarter turn, and read off the envelope
10. Transforms in two and three dimensions: a slice of the spectrum is a shadow of the object
